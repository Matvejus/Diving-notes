
# Introduction

This course is primarily designed to provide a solid education in nitrox, though simultaneously it also will help to prepare a student for subsequent TDI continuing education programs at the technical diver level.

As most divers know, air is comprised of approximately 21% oxygen and 79% nitrogen. Actually the more precise ingredients are 20.95% oxygen, 78.08% nitrogen, and 0.97% other trace gases – however, for practical purposes the trace gases are physiologically insignificant and thus they may be considered to be the same as nitrogen, and for simplicity, most often they are just added into the nitrogen percentage; for further simplicity, typically all of these percentages also are rounded off to whole numbers. Throughout this manual this simpler definition will be used, with air defined as 21% oxygen and 79% nitrogen.

Strictly speaking, nitrox is any combination of nitrogen and oxygen gases. From the perspective of sport diving, the term nitrox is most commonly used as an abbreviated designation for enriched air nitrox, which is a breathing mixture containing more than 21% oxygen, and thus less than 79% nitrogen.

The TDI Nitrox course specifically addresses the use of nitrox mixes in the range of 22% to 40% oxygen. Over the years this has proven to be the most versatile and appropriate range of nitrox mixes, for traditional sport diving activities.

Nitrox, as a breathing mix, is not a new concept. There are historical references to various oxygen-nitrogen breathing mixes dating back to the 1800’s. A published discussion of nitrox appeared in 1943, suggesting its use to reduce the possibility of decompression sickness. The US Navy introduced its nitrox procedures and tables in 1959. NOAA (US National Oceanographic and Atmospheric Administration) launched its nitrox program in 1970, and in 1979 nitrox was included in the second edition of the NOAA Diving Manual. Then a new breed of specialized diver training organizations started to appear in 1985, for the first time offering nitrox training to sport divers.

Initially some of the older recreational training organizations opposed the introduction of nitrox to the sport diving community, perhaps because it was not yet well understood by those outside of the Navy and NOAA. Early nitrox training organizations were actually banned from a major dive industry event in 1991. But sentiments quickly changed, opponents became proponents, and by 1995 nitrox was endorsed by most of the recreational training organizations.

# Principles of pressure

When it comes to physics, nitrox divers are primarily concerned with the effects of pressure, and the manner in which any change in pressure affects the behavior of their breathing gas. Pressure is relatively constant at the surface, where subtle changes in pressure may be almost unnoticeable; conversely, significant changes in pressure can occur rapidly underwater, and the effects are often dramatic.

## Ambient pressure

Pressure is the force, or weight, pressing upon a diver, an object, or a gas. At the surface, a person experiences the weight of the Earth’s atmosphere, pressing downward. In the Metric measurement system this weight is defined as one barometric unit, commonly abbreviated as 1 bar; it is equivalent to one kilogram of force per square centimeter. In the Imperial measurement system this weight is defined as one atmosphere, commonly abbreviated as 1 atm; it is equivalent to 14.7 pounds per square inch. When consulting various Metric-Imperial conversion tables, the reader may notice that there is a slight difference between 1 bar and 1 atm; nevertheless, for practical use, these two values may be considered as equal.

Upon descent, a diver immediately experiences increasing pressure, due to the additional weight of the water now pressing downward. Water is much denser than air, and has significantly greater weight; thus a minor change in depth can have a pronounced impact upon pressure. Depth may be measured in metres of seawater, abbreviated as m, or in feet of seawater, abbreviated as ft. For every 10 m / 33 ft in depth, the pressure increases by 1 bar / 1 atm. While at the surface, the weight of the overhead atmosphere is the total pressure exerted upon a diver. While underwater, the diver experiences a total pressure equal to the weight of the overhead atmosphere combined with the weight of the overhead water. Total pressure is sometimes referred to as absolute pressure or, more commonly, ambient pressure.

Rather than thinking about depth, per se, it is critical for the reader to start thinking in terms of pressure. The important relationship between depth and pressure, addressed above, is further illustrated in Table 1 (below).

### Boyle's Law
[[Gas Laws and physiology]]

In understanding nitrox, the first principle of physics that should be considered is Boyle’s Law, which defines the effects of ambient pressure upon a diver’s breathing gas. 

Robert Boyle (Irish natural philosopher, 1627-1692) studied the effects of pressure upon gas inside a flexible container, and determined that any change in pressure, applied upon a gas, will result in a corresponding and inversely proportional change in the volume of that gas. Simply stated, if the pressure is doubled, the gas will decrease in volume by one half; and if the pressure is decreased by one half, the volume will double. Boyle’s Law may be expressed mathematically as Formula 5 (below); the subscript numerals in this formula are simply used to designate the beginning and ending values for pressure and volume.
#### Pressure - Volume Changes
P1V1 = P2V2

Though the volume of a gas may change in response to changing pressure, the number of gas molecules within the flexible container will remain constant. As the volume decreases, the gas molecules will be forced closer together, rendering the gas more dense; as the volume increases, the gas molecules will spread out, rendering the gas less dense. This relationship of volume and density is also inversely proportional.

![[Pasted image 20260316083351.png]]
There is an important aspect of Boyle’s Law that has thus far been implied, but not specifically stated – in a gas-filled flexible container, the pressure of the gas (inside the container, pressing outward) will be equal to the surrounding ambient pressure (outside the container, pressing inward); it is this balancing of pressures that causes the gas to expand and contract. 

Of course Boyle’s Law is only applicable to gases confined within some sort of flexible container (or other semi-open container, such as an inverted drinking glass), where the gas is effectively confined yet simultaneously exposed to pressure. Boyle’s Law has no effect on any gas contained within a sealed rigid container, such as a scuba cylinder, and thus the air within the cylinder is not impacted by the principles of Boyle’s Law so long as it remains inside the cylinder. 

The tissues of the diver’s body are largely non-compressible liquid, and thus are not directly impacted by the principles of Boyle’s Law. However the lungs and connected air spaces within the body are, in fact, one large and flexible gas-filled container. As a diver inhales a breath of gas from the cylinder, that gas now becomes subject to Boyle’s Law at the very instant that it exits the regulator and enters the air spaces of the diver’s body.

==In the context of the TDI Nitrox Course the most important issue, related to Boyle’s Law, is that the pressure of the breathing gas, inside a diver’s lungs during a dive, will be equal to the ambient pressure surrounding the diver.==

### Dalton's law
[[Gas Laws and physiology]]

In understanding nitrox, the second principle of physics that should be considered is Dalton’s Law, which provides a means of effectively segregating and later manipulating the individual components of a diver’s breathing mix. 

John Dalton (British chemist-physicist, 1776-1844) took Boyle’s work another step forward, examining issues related to the pressure of individual gases, within a mixture of multiple gases; he identified the principles of partial pressure, now known as Dalton’s Law.

Within the total pressure of a gas mixture (like air or nitrox), each individual gas (oxygen and nitrogen) has its own specific pressure; this is referred to as the partial pressure of the individual gas.

As previously discussed, pressure is measured in terms of atmospheric pressure, expressed as bar or atm; the same applies to partial pressure. ==Dalton found that the total pressure, exerted by a mixture, is equal to the sum of the partial pressures exerted by all gases within that mixture. Further, each component gas accounts for its fraction of the total pressure, in direct proportion to the fraction of that gas present within the total mixture. In simpler terms, air consists of 21% oxygen and 79% nitrogen; therefore the partial pressure of oxygen is equal to 21% of the total pressure of air, and the partial pressure of nitrogen is equal to 79% of the total pressure of air. ==

Another important principle, from Dalton’s research, is that a confined gas will spread out evenly through all available space (moving from any area of higher partial pressure, to equalize with any area of lower partial pressure); in the process, it will effectively co-mingle with all other gases confined within that space, resulting in a uniform gas mixture irrespective of any individual partial pressures.

In addition to considering total pressure, Dalton’s Law now provides the diver an opportunity to examine the partial pressure exerted by an individual gas within a multi-gas mixture; and while the fraction of that individual component gas will remain constant within the mixture, its partial pressure can vary widely depending upon the ambient pressure. Consider the following:

• As demonstrated in Table 4, in a flexible container filled with air at 40 m / 132 ft, the partial pressure of oxygen is 1.05 atm / bar; this is roughly equivalent to 100% oxygen at the surface.

• Similarly in Table 4, in a flexible container filled with air at 40 m / 132 ft, the partial pressure of nitrogen is 3.95 bar / atm; this is almost four times greater than 100% nitrogen at the surface.

The principles of Dalton’s Law lie at the core of the nitrox concept – though ambient pressure will always remain fixed for a given depth, by simply decreasing the level of nitrogen within the breathing mix, it is possible to diminish the physiological effects of nitrogen at that depth.

### Henry's law


William Henry (British chemist & medical physician, 1775-1836) examined the solubility of gases in liquids, and discovered that the specific quantity (mass) of a gas, which will be dissolved into a liquid, is dependent upon two factors: (1) the partial pressure of the gas; and (2) the coefficient of that gas in a particular liquid.

==According to Henry’s Law, when partial pressure is increased, additional gas will be dissolved into the liquid. In those situations where the gas chemically interacts in some way with the liquid, then the interaction typically will be accelerated in response to the increased partial pressure. In other situations where the gas is inert within the liquid, the increased partial pressure will result in additional gas being deposited into the liquid and simply held there in solution; when partial pressure is decreased, some of the previously dissolved inert gas will come out of solution and return to its gaseous state.

Normally a gas moves into and out of solution at the point where it comes into contact with the liquid (that is, at the surface of the liquid, or at a permeable membrane separating the gas from the liquid). Normally this movement is not instantaneous, and instead it will take some period of time for the process to be completed. The actual rate, at which a gas moves into or out of solution, will vary with the particular gas and the particular liquid, as well as the pressure gradient (which is the difference between the gaseous and dissolved partial pressures).

So long as total pressure remains constant, any sudden change in the partial pressure of an individual gas, within the total gas mixture, will merely accelerate the on-gassing or off-gassing process. However, when the circumstances involve a significant and / or relatively rapid decrease in the total pressure exerted upon a liquid, it may cause some of the dissolved inert gas to quickly come out of solution while still within the liquid, creating bubbles.

==In terms of quantity, gas solubility is directly proportional to partial pressure; that is, when the partial pressure of a gas is doubled, the maximum amount of gas that can be dissolved into the liquid is also doubled.

# Physiological Effects of Pressure

## Overview of Gas Dynamics

Various gases may continually travel between the lungs and the tissues, transported by the blood, moving from areas of higher partial pressure to areas of lower partial pressure. In effect, in response to the laws of physics, these gases are continually seeking to equalize their own partial pressures in all available areas.

Gas exchange occurs in the alveoli of the lungs, which are interconnected with the pulmonary capillaries of the bloodstream. When the partial pressure of any gas is higher in the lungs, than the partial pressure of the same gas dissolved in the blood, additional gas will pass through the permeable membrane of the alveoli and enter into solution in the blood. It will then be transported by the blood throughout the body, and upon reaching a location where the dissolved partial pressure is lower in the tissue than in the blood, the dissolved gas will then move out of the blood and into that tissue. 

When the partial pressure of a dissolved gas, in the tissues, is greater than the partial pressure of that gas in the lungs, the gas will move in the opposite direction; it will be picked up from the tissues by the blood, and will be transported back to the lungs, where it will now come out of solution as it passes through the permeable membrane of the alveoli, returning to its gaseous state within the air space of the lungs.

## Nitrogen Dynamics

Nitrogen is physiologically inert, in that it is not utilized in any fashion by the body; nevertheless, because of its presence in the inhaled air, some amount of nitrogen naturally gets absorbed into the body tissues.

At the surface, ambient pressure remains constant at 1 bar / 1 atm. Accordingly the partial pressure of nitrogen, in air that is inhaled into the lungs, also remains constant at a PN2 of 0.79 bar / 0.79 atm. With sufficient time spent at the surface, there is an opportunity for the partial pressure of the gaseous nitrogen in the lungs to equalize with the partial pressure of the dissolved nitrogen in the tissues. Once this process is completed, the tissues are considered to be saturated with nitrogen, as they have now absorbed all of the nitrogen that is possible under these circumstances.

When the diver descends, there is a dramatic increase in the ambient pressure surrounding the diver and a corresponding increase in the total pressure in the diver’s lungs, raising the PN2 in the lungs beyond the pressure of the dissolved nitrogen in the tissues. Naturally, because of this pressure gradient, the diver begins “on-gassing” additional nitrogen. The amount of additional nitrogen, which will be absorbed during the dive, will be dependent upon both the depth (which creates the pressure gradient) and the duration of the dive. And because of the varying characteristics of the tissues themselves, different tissues will absorb nitrogen at different rates.

When the diver ascends at the end of the dive, there is an equally dramatic decrease in the ambient pressure surrounding the diver and a corresponding decrease in the total pressure in the diver’s lungs, now lowering the PN2 in the lungs below the pressure of the additional nitrogen that was dissolved into the tissues during the dive. Because of this reversal in the pressure gradient, the diver now begins the process of “off-gassing” the additional nitrogen, and this process continues back on the surface, until such time as the pressure of the dissolved nitrogen has returned to its normal surface pressure.


## DCS

The body has tolerance for some degree of super-saturation, in regards to any dissolved inert gas. Such a situation is encountered at the end of every dive, where the diver returns to the surface with an increased level of nitrogen in the tissues (an amount that now exceeds normal surface saturation levels).

There is a limit, however, in the extent to which super-saturation can be readily tolerated within the body. Managing this exposure, to elevated levels of absorbed nitrogen, is always the foremost concern in the dive planning process. Unfortunately, due to the varying characteristics of different types of tissues, there exists no simple and practical formula by which a diver can readily calculate this limit. Thus divers routinely rely upon dive tables and/or personal dive computers for all such calculations.

Dehydration is frequently cited as a predisposing factor for DCS; it adversely influences the body’s overall functional efficiency, which in turn impacts various mechanisms, including the on-gassing and off-gassing of nitrogen.

An elevated level of carbon dioxide also may promote the onset of DCS. Carbon dioxide is a by-product of metabolism, and its output is determined by the body’s workload. Diving itself is a physical activity. Additional physical demands are placed upon a diver by cold water, strenuous activity, and other stressful conditions. Factors such as older age, diminished health and fitness, and fatigue may adversely impact the body’s ability to efficiently deal with physical demands, and thus further increase workload. Poor equipment maintenance and related performance issues, particularly involving the regulator, also may contribute to workload. 

DCS is directly related to the level of nitrogen, dissolved into the tissues during a dive, which then exceeds the body’s tolerance limits upon ascent. Managing nitrogen exposure obviously is a concern to all divers, and while breathing air within recreational depths, it actually is the primary focus of the dive planning process. Dive tables and dive computers assist a diver in managing nitrogen levels, and thus assist in minimizing the risk of DCS. In light of the various factors that may influence a diver’s susceptibility to DCS, no dive table or dive computer can completely guarantee a risk-free dive, in terms of DCS. Accordingly, divers are frequently advised to dive conservatively.

## Oxygen Dynamics

Oxygen is a vital ingredient in the body’s metabolism. It is obtained from inhaled air. Oxygen naturally moves from the lungs, into the blood and through the body to the tissues, based solely upon its partial pressure. However, unlike the dynamics of nitrogen, the movement of oxygen tends to occur in one direction only – the tissues will rarely reach a dissolved PO2 that comes close to matching the gaseous PO2 in the lungs because, as soon as the oxygen reaches the tissues, it is quickly employed in the metabolic process.

Metabolism basically uses oxygen at a cellular level to “burn” the body’s fuel (derived from ingested food), and in the process produces carbon dioxide as a by-product. The partial pressure of the dissolved carbon dioxide, in the tissues, is now greater than the gaseous partial pressure of the carbon dioxide in the lungs; so the carbon dioxide naturally moves out of the tissues, via the blood, to the lungs where it is exhaled.

Water is another by-product of metabolism, and the body eliminates excess water through urination. Coincidentally, the body’s breathing reflex is not primarily triggered by the body’s need for additional oxygen; instead it is prompted by the build up of carbon dioxide in the blood. The body’s output of carbon dioxide fluctuates widely, depending upon workload; as previously discussed, increased physical demands will necessitate increased metabolism, which in turn will increase the output of carbon dioxide.

## CNS toxicity

The body can tolerate some degree of over-oxygenation, and in some cases it may actually be beneficial, as demonstrated by the fact that 100% oxygen is used routinely in emergency and therapeutic medical applications. During such uses, the partial pressure of the oxygen is raised in the lungs, which in turn raises the level of oxygen that is distributed to the tissues and organs, accelerating metabolism and stimulating other healing processes. Naturally there are limits to the body’s tolerance to such over-oxygenation.

When a person is exposed to oxygen at significantly elevated partial pressures, it can begin to interfere with the function of the central nervous system. In the worst case scenario, it basically “short circuits” the central nervous system, resulting in convulsions. In terms of diving, there is always a significant risk of fatality if convulsions occur while underwater, as the diver will likely lose the regulator from his mouth, leading to the clear possibility of drowning.

In some circumstances, symptoms of CNS oxygen toxicity may first manifest as visual disturbances, euphoria, nausea, tinnitus (ringing in the ears), tingling or twitching of the muscles (especially the facial muscles), irritability (or sense of apprehension), dizziness, and dyspnea (difficulty breathing). However, in other circumstances, sudden convulsions may well be the first and only detectable symptom related to the onset of CNS oxygen toxicity; there may be no prior warning at all, in the form of less severe symptoms. ## Pulmonary oxygen toxicity

In some circumstances, long-term exposure to elevated levels of oxygen partial pressures may lead to pulmonary oxygen toxicity, sometimes referred to as whole body toxicity. Higher than normal levels of oxygen tend to irritate the lining of the lungs and dry the surfactant, which is the inner mucus coating that protects and lubricates the alveoli in the lungs. When the surfactant is dried out, the alveoli may collapse and surfaces may stick together, inhibiting the effective exchange of gases between the lungs and the bloodstream. Pulmonary oxygen toxicity is typically characterized by pain or discomfort in the chest, and by a dry, hacking, nonproductive cough. This condition may take hours or longer to develop, from exposure levels that may be less than those normally associated with CNS oxygen toxicity. Once the oxygen exposure returns to more normal levels, the body often is able to self-correct this condition, and the symptoms normally begin to alleviate within about 24 hours.

# Basic Dive Planning Using Tables

## Haldane's decompression model

Haldane constructed a mathematical model involving multiple theoretical tissue compartments; though these compartments did not directly correspond to actual body tissues, he hypothesized that, taken together, they would mimic the overall behavior of nitrogen within a diver’s body. Haldane assigned a half-time to each tissue compartment, as the rate at which it would absorb and release nitrogen. Half-time is the time, in minutes, for a compartment to go halfway from its beginning tissue pressure to saturation at a new depth, in an exponential progression – the actual quantity (mass) of gas being dissolved or released would vary over time, initially occurring at a higher rate but then progressively slowing as the pressure gradient narrowed; at the end of six half-times, for practical purposes, the compartment is considered to be saturated.

Haldane combined all of this information into a complex mathematical computation, or algorithm, by which he projected a schedule of depth and time limits that constituted the first dive tables (used by the navy from 1907 to 1956). Though theoretical in nature, time has largely proven his methodology to be valid.

## Modern dive tables

Despite their individual differences, all tables are designed to assist the diver in determining the maximum allowable bottom time at a given depth with a given breathing mix (typically air), the amount of additional nitrogen absorbed into the tissues during a dive (typically expressed in terms of a pressure group), the off-gassing of nitrogen during a surface interval between dives (typically expressed in terms of a new pressure group), and the amount of residual nitrogen that must be accounted for in any subsequent dive (typically expressed as residual nitrogen time, for a given depth).

### US Navy tables

The US Navy dive tables have long been recognized as the “universal” modern dive tables. They have seen widespread use among military, scientific, commercial, and sport divers worldwide. They remain the standard tables currently utilized by NOAA, and they have been adopted by a number of diver training organizations.

# Nitrox concepts

At its simplest, nitrox was developed as a means of reducing the level of nitrogen to which a diver is exposed during a dive. As introduced in Chapter One, nitrox is a breathing mixture containing more than 21% oxygen, and thus less than 79% nitrogen. In sport diving applications, the most versatile nitrox mixtures are those in the range of EAN22 to EAN40.

Nitrox, with its lower percentage of nitrogen, potentially offers several advantages over air, depending upon the manner in which it is used by the diver:

• Nitrox may provide an effective means of safely extending the diver’s allowable bottom time, compared to breathing air at that same depth.

• Nitrox may provide an effective means of safely reducing the required minimum surface interval between repetitive dives, compared to breathing air for that same series of dives; by reducing minimum surface intervals, the diver also may be able to complete more dives within a given period of time.

• Nitrox may provide an effective means of further reducing a diver’s exposure to the risk of decompression sickness on a given dive, if the diver simply adheres to the same depth and time limits that would be applicable when breathing air for that dive.

Again, nitrox contains a higher fraction of oxygen, compared to the air that traditionally has been used in diving activities. The sole purpose, in adding this additional oxygen to the breathing mix, is to reduce the level of nitrogen to which the diver is exposed during a particular dive. Other than replacing some of the nitrogen in the mix, the additional oxygen itself adds no other benefit, and in fact it adds some additional concerns related to the possibility of CNS (central nervous system) oxygen toxicity. Luckily for the diver, managing his oxygen exposures is quite similar to managing his nitrogen exposures; it simply adds another step to the nitrox dive planning process.

## Extended bottom times

Nitrox may offer several advantages for the diver. The first and perhaps most obvious advantage is that nitrox effectively extends a diver’s no decompression limit at any given depth, providing him with the opportunity for additional bottom time.

According to the US Navy dive tables, when a dive is made with air to a depth of 18 m / 60 ft, the NDL is 60 minutes. If the same dive was made with EAN40, the NDL would be 163 minutes – yes, that’s more than double the no-deco limit of air ! Obviously this is an extreme example, and a diver certainly should not expect similar results in all situations; but an appropriate nitrox mix can be used to safely increase bottom times, to some extent, within all traditional recreational depths.

## Reduced Surface Intervals

When considering nitrox, divers sometimes focus exclusively upon its extended no-deco limits (perhaps because this issue always seems to get their attention). But what happens to all that allowable bottom time, when it isn’t actually used during a dive ? Well, in effect, the diver gets some “credit” for it in his surface intervals. 

The use of nitrox, with its reduced fraction of nitrogen, means that a diver will be absorbing less nitrogen on any given dive, compared to using air for the same dive. When a diver on-gasses less nitrogen during a dive, naturally he will have less nitrogen to off-gas after the dive. Then, when he returns to the water after his surface interval, he naturally will have less residual nitrogen in his body from the prior dive, compared to him having done that prior dive on air.

## Risk of DCS

Some divers may prefer to use nitrox as their breathing mix while still adhering to the depth and time limits for air. They are neither looking to extend their bottom times, nor to shorten their surface intervals. They have simply concluded that, with a lower dose of nitrogen on any given dive, logically they should be exposed to a somewhat lower risk of DCS. This approach is sometimes referred to as using nitrox for its physiological advantage. 

Though at times some have questioned the validity of this approach, the NOAA Diving Manual (fourth edition, 2001) clearly appears to endorse the use of nitrox in this manner. 

It is important to point out that nitrox is not a magic bullet to manage DCS. There is no way that the use of any nitrox mix can guarantee a risk-free dive, in terms of DCS; there simply are too many variables involved. There is only one known method to completely eliminate the possibility of DCS – that is, do not dive at all. All divers must accept the risk of DCS, and then plan and execute their dives to most effectively manage this risk.

## Will Nitrox Improve gas consumption

Occasionally a diver may surmise that, because nitrox has an increased oxygen content and thus more oxygen in each inhaled breath, perhaps a nitrox diver may not have to breathe as much during a dive; therefore his gas consumption should improve simply because he’s using nitrox. 

Unfortunately, this is false. The body’s breathing reflex is primarily triggered by the build up of carbon dioxide in the blood (not by a lack of oxygen). Carbon dioxide is a by-product of metabolism, and the output of carbon dioxide is determined by the metabolic rate, which in turn is determined by the body’s workload. As long as all other circumstances remain constant, the workload during a dive while breathing nitrox will be identical to the workload while breathing air. If a diver finds that his cylinder of air lasts about 60 minutes at a certain depth, he will find that the same cylinder filled with nitrox also will last about 60 minutes at the same depth.

1. Nitrox is a breathing mixture containing more than 21% oxygen, and less than 79% nitrogen. 
2. The amount of additional nitrogen, absorbed during a dive, is directly dependent upon the partial pressure of nitrogen. 
3. Besides replacing some of the nitrogen in the breathing mix, the additional oxygen, in nitrox, provides several additional benefits for a diver. False 
4. In thinking of nitrogen and oxygen as drugs, the effect of each drug will be dependent upon the dose (determined by its partial pressure), combined with the duration of exposure (determined by dive time). 
5. Nitrox may be an effective means of extending no-decompression limits. True

# Chapter 6 - Nitrox Dive planning, part 1, managing Nitrogen Exposures

## Equivalent Air depth

When using nitrox, the diver is exposed to a reduced dose (lower partial pressure) of nitrogen, compared to air. In effect, when using nitrox at a specific depth over a specific period of time, it is the equivalent of this diver breathing air at a shallower depth for the same period of time. By accurately calculating this equivalent shallower depth, it is then possible to simply use this shallower depth in place of the actual depth, with any air dive tables, for all standard dive calculations. This concept is known as equivalent air depth (abbreviated as EAD).

![[Pasted image 20260317111658.png]]
The EAD for any nitrox mix, at any depth, can be manually calculated, by first creating a ratio where the fraction of nitrogen in nitrox is divided by the faction of nitrogen in air, then multiplying that ratio by the ambient pressure expressed in m / ft, then converting that figure back to depth. This mathematical procedure is depicted in Metric Formula 7 and Imperial Formula 8 (below).

Metric: Equivalent Air Depth EAD = [ (FN2 / .79) x (D + 10) ] – 10

0.63/0.79 x 45 - 10


Using EAD with air dive tables

In nitrox dive planning, the EAD information (once determined) is simply applied to a standard set of air dive tables; the tables plot the dose of nitrogen experienced by the diver, based upon depth and time. In practice, a diver certainly may use any air dive tables with which he is comfortable. The TDI version of the US Navy dive tables again are used here for demonstration (depicted right; another copy of these tables also appears in the appendix of this manual, and “hard” copies of these tables are available through your TDI instructor or dive center). When using nitrox, the no-decompression limit is calculated as usual on the air dive tables, simply with the EAD used in place of the actual depth.

Example:
When using EAN36 at 15 m / 50 ft, the EAD is 12 m / 40 ft, and thus the applicable no-decompression limit is now 163 minutes. The ending pressure group, for any dive, is also calculated in the usual manner, again with the EAD used in place of the actual depth.

Example:
After completing a dive with EAN36 for 60 minutes at an EAD of 12 m / 40 ft, a diver will surface in pressure group G. Surface interval credit is calculated in the normal fashion (after all, regardless of the mix that a diver may use at depth, he will still be breathing air back on the surface during the surface interval). 

Example:
Surfacing in pressure group G, followed by a two hour surface interval, the diver will then be in pressure group E. In planning for a repetitive dive, the diver may use the same or a different nitrox mix; in either case, the EAD is again used to determine the residual nitrogen time. 

IMPORTANT:
In calculating residual nitrogen time, the diver should base these calculations on the nitrox mix that will be used on the repetitive dive. 

Example:
After completing his surface interval, a diver in pressure group E, planning another dive with EAN40 to an EAD of 9 m / 30 ft, will have a residual nitrogen time of 63 minutes. The residual nitrogen time is then added to the actual time at the EAD, to determine the ending pressure group.

Example:
A diver in pressure group E, completing a dive to an EAD of 9 m / 30 ft for 60 minutes, will have a total bottom time of 123 minutes (60 actual plus 63 residual), and will surface in pressure group I.

• If more than one dive is made at a PO2 of 1.6 bar / atm, a suggested surface interval of at least 90 minutes is advised between dives.

• If, however, one or more dives in a 24-hour period have reached the time limit for a normal single exposure, a minimum surface interval of two hours is recommended before resuming diving.

• If diving in a 24-hour period reaches the daily time limit, a minimum surface interval of 12 hours is recommended before resuming diving.

A diver, in reasonably good health and diving under reasonably acceptable conditions, may confidently plan a dive to the maximum PO2 of 1.6 bar / atm for the maximum time of 45 minutes. When planning only a single dive for that day, this may well be the appropriate parameters for this diver to use, in order to maximize the benefits provided by nitrox. When planning a series of repetitive dives for that same day, under the same conditions, the same parameters may still be applied (at a PO2 of 1.6 bar / atm, for 45 minutes on each dive), however the diver must recognize (as indicated above) that a minimum surface interval of two hours will be required between those dives, because each is at 100% of the single dive limit; and even if the diver significantly reduced the duration of each dive in this series, a minimum surface interval of 90 minutes would still be required between these dives, because of the multiple exposures at a PO2 of 1.6 bar / atm.

There are a number of reasons that a diver may appropriately elect to reduce his PO2 exposure level to something less than the maximum of 1.6 bar / atm. Several examples follow:

• A diver may wish to add a degree of conservatism to his overall dive planning, especially when adverse conditions may be encountered, or because he simply wants to leave a bit of a margin in the event of “diver error” or some other contingency.

• The diver might want to extend the duration of a dive beyond the maximum time limit of 45 minutes (which is the maximum allowed at a PO2 of 1.6 bar / atm), perhaps to 60 minutes or longer.

• Imposed time constrains, beyond the diver’s immediate control, may preclude the previously-specified minimum surface intervals; such a situation could be encountered while boat diving in a resort setting, where the boat’s schedule only provides an opportunity for a relatively brief surface interval during a two-tank trip.

• The diver may might to participate in more dives during a single day than would be allowed at the PO2 exposure level of 1.6 bar / atm, such as during a typical live-aboard dive vacation where four, five, or even six dives per day are not uncommon.

## Calculating Cumulative CNS Exposures

As addressed above, NOAA has established limits for each PO2 level, within the range of 0.6 to 1.6 bar / atm. As these limits are time-based and intended to control exposure to the risk of CNS oxygen toxicity, this schedule of limits is sometimes referred to as the “CNS clock.” Exposures must be monitored on a per-dive basis and, when participating in repetitive dives, these exposures also must be monitored on a daily (24 hour) basis. For example, at a PO2 of 1.6 bar / atm the single dive CNS exposure limit is 45 minutes, and the daily CNS exposure limit is 150 minutes.

When dealing with a series of dives where the PO2 varies from dive to dive, a different thought process is required. Rather than considering actual minutes of time, it is more helpful instead to view the duration of each dive in terms of its percentage of the allowable CNS limit. The percentages from individual dives in a series then are simply added together, to arrive at a cumulative CNS exposure.

The mathematical procedure is relatively straight-forward for calculating the percentage of the single dive and daily limits, to which a diver has been exposed. A diver who completes a 30 minute dive at a PO2 of 1.6 bar / atm will have been exposed to 67% of his single dive CNS limit, as calculated by dividing the 30 minute actual dive time by the 45 minute limit; this same diver also will have been exposed to 20% of his daily limit, as calculated by dividing the 30 minute actual dive time by the 150 minute limit.

![[Pasted image 20260318092039.png]]

In the above example, the reader will note that the percentage of exposure was calculated in terms of both the single dive CNS limit and the daily CNS limit. This is based upon another important consideration – single dive limit may be somewhat of a misnomer, as in some cases this same CNS limit will equally apply as the cumulative limit in a series of dives. When performing repetitive nitrox dives separated by only relatively brief surface intervals (such as in those examples cited above, with a two-tank boat trip in a resort setting, or on a live-aboard dive boat), most authorities opine that each successive dive is actually a continuation of the prior dive, in terms of CNS exposure (basically, the “CNS clock” just keeps ticking, from one dive into another). In such a case, it is recommended that the percentages of exposure from each dive in this series should be added together, and that the specified minimum two-hour surface interval should be applied whenever the combined exposure approaches 100% of the single dive CNS limit.

On occasion a diver may question whether he receives any credit, for time spent at the surface between dives, in reference to his oxygen exposures and related CNS limits. In effect this credit is already included in the NOAA oxygen exposure limits; that’s why, with the prerequisite minimum surface intervals, the NOAA daily limits are typically longer than the single dive limits. Naturally, since this is the method that was developed and adopted by NOAA for dealing with surface interval credit, it is also the method that is detailed in this manual.

It should be noted that surface interval credit generally appears to be “earned” at a somewhat slower rate for cumulative oxygen exposures, than it is for cumulative nitrogen exposures. Several references cite 90 minutes as the incremental interval for purposes of calculating significant surface credit, and such appears consistent with the NOAA methodology.
