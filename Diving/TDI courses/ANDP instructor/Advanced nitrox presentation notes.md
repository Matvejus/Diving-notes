Objective: This chapter tells the history of decompression models and introduces currently used ones. Explains the differences between them and how they work.

#### Haldane's decompression model
John Scott Haldane understood that as a diver descends, his body absorbs inert gas, and as he ascends, the inert gas escapes. Through experimentation, Haldane developed the theory that liquids such as blood can withstand a certain level of super-saturation without forming bubbles. 

Supersaturation is when the pressure of gas inside the tissues is higher than the ambient pressure

He called this maximum level of super-saturation the “critical limit.” Haldane’s original decompression model was designed to predict the behavior of inert gasses in five tissue compartments and was based on the theory that maximizing the level of super-saturation without exceeding the critical limit would achieve the greatest off-gassing efficiency.

What are those compartments? 5, 10, 20, 40, 75

• It was based on the theory that maximizing super-saturation without exceeding the critical limit achieves the greatest off gassing efficiency. 

• Tables encouraged ascending as much as possible without exceeding the critical limit.

#### Neo-Haldanean Models

**Doppler** testing has proven the existence of silent bubbles even on dives that obeyed Haldane’s critical limits. After this discovery, many scientists were of the opinion that Haldane’s original “dissolved gas model” had worked quite well throughout the years and that only minor tweaks were necessary.

Doppler testing is using ultrasound system which use high-frequency sound waves to detect movement in the body. While blood cells scatter these waves, gas bubbles produce a much stronger signal, allowing the system to detect and estimate the number of bubbles in the bloodstream. The Doppler system uses ultrasound transducers placed on the body to detect blood flow. Although it sounds like a microphone is listening to the blood vessels through headphones, the sound is actually created from reflected ultrasound signals. When gas bubbles are present, you hear what sounds like little ‘clicks’ or ‘pops.’

Pyle stops:
Pyle was not a tech diver, he was fish nerd. He used to make a lot of dives to around 60m to collect fish and bring them back to the surface. He started noticing that after some dives he felt less fatigue and started collecting and analyzing dive data. After some time he figured out that on the dives where he had to make a  deep stop to release the pressure from fish's swimbladder for a couple of minutes.

Pyle's method:

1. Calculate the decompression profile as you would for a regular dive using the same software. 
2. take the distance from your bottom depth to the first decompression stop, middle of this distance will be your first 2-3 minutes stop. 
3) If the distance between your first deep safety stop and your first “required” stop is greater than 9 m / 30 ft, then add a second deep safety stop at the midpoint between the first deep safety stop and the first required stop. 
4) Repeat as necessary until there is less than 9 m / 30 ft between your last deep safety stop and the first required safety stop.

We don't dive like that anymore. If you would use this method, your first deep stop might be at depth where instead of decompressing nitrogen your slow tissues would still be ongassing. 

#### Gradient factors

Eric Baker applied gradient factors to Buhlmans m values. 
According to buhlman algorith we would ascent to the m value of the controlling tissue. To be more conservative we can apply gradient factors to the m-values that would give use shallower depths for the first stops and longer stops for the last stop.

Generally speaking, Baker’s gradient factors control how close a diver may come to any of Bühlmann’s allowable M-values. Gradient factors are expressed as percentages. A gradient factor of 100% would allow a diver to ascend until the M-value was reached, while a gradient factor of 50% would only allow a diver to ascend until 50% of the M-value was reached.

Baker designed gradient factors to be used in sets of two. The first number is known as the gradient factor low or GF(Lo) and is used to control the first deep stop. The second number is known as the gradient factor high or GF(Hi). The GF(Hi) controls the percentage of M-value that any tissue is allowed to reach upon surfacing.

VGM - VRX computer

For a few years, divers have used different gradient factors to achieve this change in style, manually adjusting gradient factors of Haldane models to suit the dive they are planning. VGM does a similar thing, but modifies the over pressure tolerances of the different tissues that control the various stop depths and durations. VGM modifies these tolerances based on increased depth and bottom time so that the diver does not have to. The equivalent gradient factor is shown during and after each dive. This gradient factor will change depending on the bottom depth and time for each dive. The user can further manually adjust the factors modifying the different tissues to achieve a decompression that suits his or her experience and body conditions.

The major drawback of gradient factors is that the factors applied need to be adjusted for each depth/time exposure. For example, if you used the same 20/80 gradient factor for an 80 m dive, on a 30 m dive you might have an excess of decompression in shallow water because we know from experience that a gradient factor of close to 100/100 is reliable for this shallow water dive. 

What does this mean? First, it is not necessarily appropriate to apply one gradient factor to a range of dive depths. What works deep may not work shallow. Secondly, it means just applying gradient factors in the first place may be too coarse a solution. Just drawing a straight line between the M value points and assuming the mid-water decompression follows this linear approach may not work. So how do we generate a refinement?

VGM has the ability to use stochastic data and historically ‘proven’ decompression values in an algorithm and automatically adjust it for a range of depth/time exposure scenarios.

In summary, a VGM profile has the effect of padding the stops at certain decompression points while reducing stop times at others for a specific exposure scenario. What’s more, it changes them again automatically for different exposure scenarios and allows varying degrees of user input dependent on the application.

#### Dual phase models
Instead of asking: > "How much dissolved gas is present?" 
they ask: > "Will existing bubbles grow to dangerous sizes?"

Hills thermodynamic approach attempted to predict the behavior of both dissolved gas in solution and gas in a free state (bubbles). This new modeling concept was the birth of what we now call Dual Phase or Bubble Models.

To truly comprehend bubble models, you must first understand what drives bubbles to grow and shrink. In order for a bubble to shrink, the pressure pushing in on the bubble must be greater than the pressure pushing out. Conversely, for a bubble to grow, the pressure pushing out must be greater than the pressure pushing in.

The pressure of the gas within a bubble constitutes the primary outward pressing force, while the pressure pressing in on a bubble is a combination of three key elements. The first is the ambient pressure, the second is the surface tension (or Laplace pressure) and the third is the pressure created by the elasticity of the tissue surrounding the bubble.

There are two ways in which a bubble may be forced to grow or shrink. The first is by altering ambient pressure, and the second is by altering the concentration of the gas inside the bubble. Altering the ambient pressure is relatively simple and can be achieved by simply ascending or descending. Unfortunately, we cannot hope to reduce a bubble’s size through ambient pressure changes alone as this would require a continual descent.

In the above example, if a bubble whose radius gave it a Laplace pressure of 1.5 ATA were introduced, it would remain stable because the pressure of gas in the bubble would equal the pressure of inert gas in the tissue. This bubble’s size would be called the “critical radius”

Dual phase (bubble) models attempt to control the size of the bubbles in a diver’s tissues. By keeping the bubbles below the critical radius, they will continue to reduce in size as the diver ascends. To accomplish this, bubble models will introduce deep stops to maintain a high ambient pressure and minimize super-saturation, thus producing a higher pressure of gas in the bubble. Once the bubble shrinks to an acceptable size by forcing the internal gas to diffuse outward, the ascent will continue.

#### Quantity of bubbles
Bubble models must account for different tissue perfusion rates and half times. Fast tissues will on-gas quickly during the descent and bottom phase of the dive, creating a higher level of super-saturation during the ascent. This in turn causes more bubbles to grow and expand quickly, hence the need for deep stops. During these deep stops, however, slower tissues will continue to on-gas and necessary decompression times at shallower stops will increase. In order to minimize decompression time, bubble models must balance the benefits and penalties of the deep stops.

#### VPM
VPM (Variable Permeability Model) Variable Permeability Model is a dual phase bubble model. It utilizes the Haldane and Schreiner equations to track tissue on and off-gassing in 16 unique tissue compartments; in addition, it works on the assumption that gas micronuclei are present in a diver’s blood and tissues prior to submerging and attempts to limit the growth of bubbles during the ascent phase of the dive.

The VPM algorithm’s assumption that gas micronuclei are present prior to a dive seems to contradict what was discussed earlier in this chapter regarding bubble behavior. As you have learned, a bubble’s internal gas pressure is a combination of the ambient pressure and its Laplace pressure; therefore, in theory, at the surface the bubble’s internal gas pressure should always exceed the surrounding gas tissue pressure and the bubble should shrink and collapse.

The theory adopted by the VPM algorithm is that the tiny bubbles are preserved by a congregation of surface active molecules, or surfactants. VPM treats the surfactant layer as if it is a boundary made from tiny balls (the tiny balls are actually molecules). In very small bubbles, the tiny balls press tightly against each other, forming an impermeable barrier. This impermeable layer does not allow the force from the surface tension to affect the internal bubble pressure; therefore, gas does not enter or leave the bubble and it becomes stable. In larger bubbles, the tiny balls are more dispersed, thus allowing gas transfer to take place. This variable permeability theory is what gives the VPM model its name.

The original VPM model attempted to eliminate the growth of the stable tiny bubbles by never allowing the gas tissue tension to exceed the internal bubble pressure during the ascent. The original model worked quite well for saturation diving, but proved too conservative for most sport and technical diving applications.

 The solution adopted for the subsequent revision of the VPM model was the incorporation of the critical volume algorithm. This approach assumes that the symptoms of DCI are generated by exceeding a critical volume of free gas rather than solely by the size or number of bubbles present. In order to control the volume of free gas in the body, this new VPM model needed to track the: 
 1) Number of bubbles that would grow 
 2) Rate at which the growing bubbles expanded 
 3) Expansion timeframe

## Chapter at a glance

1. **Haldane (1900s):** the body absorbs inert gas on descent and releases it on ascent. Stay below a "critical limit" of super-saturation and no bubbles form. Five tissue compartments.
2. **Neo-Haldanian (mid-1900s onward):** Doppler tests found silent bubbles even on "safe" dives, but the dissolved-gas idea was kept and tweaked. Tweaks: Pyle's deep stops, Baker's gradient factors, VGM.
3. **Dual phase / bubble models (1960s onward):** model gas in solution _and_ gas as free bubbles. Hills, then Yount (VPM) and Weinke (RGBM). Deep stops now come from the physics of bubbles, not from observation.

The whole chapter ends on one message: every model is theoretical, none guarantees 100% safety, so plan conservatively and listen to your body.

## Slide 4: Neo-Haldanian models and deep stops

**Slide says:** Doppler tests; initial stops performed deeper; Pyle; Pyle's method for incorporating deep stops.

**Explain:**

- **Doppler testing** detects bubbles in the blood. It showed "silent bubbles" even on dives that stayed inside Haldane's critical limits. Silent bubbles cause no symptoms, but they showed the model was incomplete.
- Most scientists said the dissolved-gas model had worked well for years and needed only small tweaks. Models that follow Haldane's assumptions (Workman's tables, US Navy tables, Bühlmann ZHL-16) are called **modified Haldanian** or **Neo-Haldanian**.
- As divers went deeper, stayed longer and used mixed gases, the main adjustment was a **deeper first stop**.
- Richard L. Pyle (a fish collector, not a decompression physiologist) noticed that after 180–220 ft dives he felt fatigue and malaise, except on days he collected fish. To release the gas in a fish's swim bladder he stopped about halfway between his deepest depth and his first decompression stop for 2–3 minutes. Adding those stops to every dive made his symptoms virtually disappear. These are called **Pyle stops**.
- Why it worked was not understood until 1989, when Pyle heard Dr. David Yount's talk: the stops were controlling the number of bubbles and how fast they grow.

**Pyle's method (use on the board):**

1. Compute a normal decompression profile with your software.
2. Find the midpoint between the start of the ascent and the first required stop. That is the first deep stop, 2–3 minutes.
3. Recalculate the profile including that stop.
4. If the gap between the deep stop and the first required stop is more than 9 m / 30 ft, add another stop at the midpoint.
5. Repeat until the gap is under 9 m / 30 ft.

_Worked example for the board:_ bottom at 90 m, first required stop at 30 m. Midpoint is 60 m (first deep stop). Gap 60 to 30 = 30 m, which is more than 9 m, so add a stop at 45 m. Gap 45 to 30 = 15 m, still more than 9 m, so add 37.5 m (about 38 m). Gap 7.5 m, which is under 9 m, done.

**Ask students:**

1. What did Doppler testing prove, and why did it matter? _(Silent bubbles exist even on dives inside Haldane's limits.)_
2. What makes a model "Neo-Haldanian"? _(It keeps Haldane's dissolved-gas assumptions with modifications.)_
3. Why were Pyle's stops met with skepticism? _(They came from observation, with no theory to explain them.)_
4. Pyle was not a physiologist. Is practical observation enough to change a model? What are the risks? _(Open discussion: works, but without theory conservatism can be inconsistent, which leads to slide 5.)_
5. Using Pyle's method: bottom at 60 m, first required stop at 21 m. Where do the deep stops go? _(Midpoint 40.5 m; gap 19.5 m is over 9 m, so next at 30.75 m; gap 9.75 m is still over 9, so add 25.9 m; gap under 9 m, done.)_

## Slide 5: Gradient factors and VGM

**Slide says:** Gradient factors; Eric Baker; applied to Bühlmann's M-values; expressed as percentages; sets of two numbers; GF(Lo) controls the first deep stop; GF(Hi) controls the M-value a tissue may reach at the surface; user adjustable; VGM; VRX computer.

**Explain:**

- Baker (an avid trimix and cave diver and an electrical engineer) saw that Pyle-style stops gave _inconsistent_ conservatism across different profiles. Why? In a normal profile the fastest tissues lead early and their M-values allow large, fast overpressure gradients on the first ascent to the first stop. That is out of proportion to the smaller gradients later, when slower tissues control. Many bubbles could form during that first ascent.
- An **M-value** is the maximum gas pressure a tissue is allowed to hold at a given depth in Bühlmann's model. A **gradient factor** is a percentage of the gap between ambient pressure and the M-value. GF 100% means ascend right up to the M-value; GF 50% means stop at half way.
- Factors come as a pair: **GF(Lo)** sets how deep the first stop is, **GF(Hi)** sets how close to the M-value any tissue may be on surfacing. Common set: **30/85**. A stop is inserted when the leading tissue reaches 30% of its M-value, and the allowed factor rises gradually to 85% at the surface.
- Divers can adjust them: _lower GF(Lo)_ gives a deeper first stop; _lower GF(Hi)_ lengthens shallow stops. Divers tune these to how they feel after the dive.
- **VGM (Variable Gradient Model):** built into VR Technology's VRX computer. It uses statistical dive data to adjust the gradient factors automatically through the dive, so the diver does not have to pick them by hand, and shows the equivalent gradient factor after each dive.

**Ask students:**

1. What problem did Baker see in Pyle's deep stops? _(Inconsistent conservatism between profiles; large early overpressure from fast tissues.)_
2. What does a gradient factor of 50% mean? _(The diver may only ascend until 50% of the way to the M-value.)_
3. In 30/85, what does each number control? _(30 = GF(Lo), first stop depth; 85 = GF(Hi), tissue loading at surface.)_
4. You feel tired after a dive with 30/85. Which number do you change and which way for deeper stops? _(Lower GF(Lo), e.g. 20/85. For longer shallow stops, lower GF(Hi).)_
5. Which is more conservative, 30/70 or 30/85? _(30/70: the surfacing M-value limit is lower.)_
6. How does VGM differ from setting gradient factors by hand? _(It adjusts them automatically based on depth and bottom time.)_

## Slide 6: Dual phase models – where they came from

**Slide says:** Behnke; silent bubbles; Brian Hills; worked with LeMessurier; Thermodynamic / Zero-super-saturation; not well received.

**Explain:**

- Behnke coined "silent bubbles" and proposed that bubbles alone do not cause DCS. What matters is the **amount of free gas** and the degree of embolism the body can tolerate.
- Most scientists stayed on Haldane's path. **Brian Hills** did not. In 1963, as a chemical engineering lecturer studying unwanted bubbles in melting nylon, he met Dr. Hugh LeMessurier, who had studied pearl divers on Thursday Island and found outcomes Haldane's theory could not explain.
- At the Kuri Bay pearl farm, two deaths prompted an official investigation. Hills and LeMessurier found divers using a system built by decades of trial and error: **much deeper stops** and a direct ascent from 7 m / 25 ft, finishing in two thirds of the US Navy table time. Problems appeared mainly when strong tides pushed divers shallower than planned.
- Hills concluded the wording of Haldane's theory did not match the equations used to build the tables, and that the deep stops were the key. He developed the **Thermodynamic** ("zero-super-saturation") approach, modeling both dissolved gas and free gas (bubbles). This was the birth of **dual phase (bubble) models**.
- It received little support because it was so different, and faded until later researchers revived it.

**Ask students:**

1. What did Behnke think actually causes DCS? _(Not the presence of bubbles, but the quantity of free gas.)_
2. What were the Kuri Bay divers doing differently, and what went wrong when it failed? _(Deeper stops, direct ascent from 7 m; trouble when tides carried them shallower than intended.)_
3. Why do you think Hills' ideas were rejected? _(Too radical; Haldane's tables were the established standard.)_
4. What two phases does "dual phase" refer to? _(Gas dissolved in solution and gas in free state, i.e. bubbles.)_

## Slides 7–11: Bubble behavior and worked pressure examples

This is the hardest part of the chapter. Teach it as one chain of reasoning: what pushes in, what pushes out, and what happens when either changes.

**Slide says:** Slide 7: ambient pressure changes, Laplace pressure. Slides 8–10: three calculations with 5 ATA, 3 ATA, 0.5 ATA Laplace pressure, 3.95 ATA tissue nitrogen. Slide 11: Laplace pressure is tied to bubble radius.

**Explain slide 7 (the forces):**

- A bubble **shrinks** if the pressure pushing in is greater than the pressure pushing out. It **grows** if the pressure pushing out is greater.
- Pushing out: the **gas pressure inside** the bubble.
- Pushing in: **ambient pressure** + **Laplace pressure** (surface tension, like the rubber skin of a balloon; the chapter folds tissue elasticity into it for simplicity).
- You can change a bubble's size two ways: change ambient pressure (ascend or descend), or change the gas concentration inside it. Ambient pressure alone cannot shrink a bubble, since that would need continual descent. We must reach the surface, so the gas inside the bubble has to leave.

**Explain slides 8–10 (three snapshots, one diver):**

```latex
P_{\text{gas in bubble}} = P_{\text{Laplace}} + P_{\text{ambient}}
```

|   |   |   |   |   |   |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|Situation|Ambient|Laplace|Gas in bubble|N₂ in tissue|Result|
|Slide 8–9: diver stays at 40 m / 130 ft (saturated on air)|5 ATA|0.5 ATA|5.5 ATA|3.95 ATA|Bubble gas pressure is higher, so gas diffuses out and the bubble **shrinks**|
|Slide 10: diver ascends fast to 20 m / 66 ft|3 ATA|0.5 ATA|3.5 ATA|3.95 ATA (no time to off-gas)|Tissue pressure is higher, so gas diffuses _into_ the bubble and it **grows**|

- Where does 3.95 come from? Air is 79% nitrogen: 0.79 ATA at the surface and 0.79 × 5 = 3.95 ATA at 5 ATA.
- The lesson: ascending too quickly drops ambient pressure faster than tissues can release gas.

**Explain slide 11 (why big bubbles keep growing):**

- Laplace pressure rises as radius falls, and falls as radius rises. So a bubble that starts to grow loses Laplace pressure, which lowers its internal gas pressure, which makes it take on even more gas. It is a runaway effect.
- Chapter diagram: tissue N₂ 3.5 ATA, ambient 2 ATA. Bubble A (Laplace 1 ATA) has 3 ATA inside, below 3.5, so it grows. Bubble B (Laplace 2 ATA) has 4 ATA inside, above 3.5, so it shrinks. A bubble with Laplace 1.5 ATA has exactly 3.5 ATA and is stable. That size is the **critical radius**.

**Ask students:**

1. Name the three things pushing in on a bubble and the one pushing out. _(In: ambient, Laplace, tissue elasticity. Out: gas pressure inside.)_
2. A diver at 4 ATA has a bubble with Laplace pressure 0.4 ATA. What is the pressure inside? _(4.4 ATA.)_
3. If tissue N₂ is 3.16 ATA (air, 4 ATA), will that bubble shrink or grow if the diver stays? _(Shrink: 4.4 is greater than 3.16.)_
4. The same diver ascends fast to 2 ATA and tissue N₂ stays 3.16. What happens? _(Inside = 2.4 ATA, below 3.16, so the bubble grows.)_
5. Why can't we shrink bubbles just by changing depth? _(We would need to keep descending; we must surface, so gas must leave the bubble.)_
6. Why is a large bubble more dangerous than a small one? _(Lower Laplace pressure, so lower internal pressure, so it keeps on-gassing and growing.)_
7. What is the critical radius? _(The size where internal gas pressure equals tissue gas pressure; smaller bubbles shrink, larger ones grow.)_

## Slides 12–14: Controlling bubble size and quantity

**Slide says:** Slide 12: dual phase models control bubble size; keep bubbles below the critical radius so they shrink on ascent. Slide 13: deep stops maintain high ambient pressure and minimize super-saturation; higher gas pressure in the bubble; ascent continues once the bubble is small enough. Slide 14: control quantity of bubbles; different tissue rates; fast tissues and deep stops; slow tissues; ascent more small bubbles.

**Explain:**

- **Size (slides 12–13).** Keep bubbles below the critical radius and they keep shrinking as the diver ascends. Deep stops hold ambient pressure high, which limits super-saturation and keeps the gas pressure inside the bubble above the tissue's, so gas diffuses _out_. Once the bubble is small enough, the ascent continues. This is why bubble models give deeper first stops than Haldanian models.
- **Quantity (slide 14).** Silent bubbles exist after nearly every dive, so bubbles alone cannot be the problem. Following Behnke, bubble models try to control the _quantity of free gas_.
- A real body has many tissues with different perfusion rates and half-times. **Fast tissues** take up gas quickly on the way down, leading to high super-saturation on ascent and many fast-growing bubbles, hence deep stops. But while you wait at deep stops, **slow tissues** keep on-gassing, so shallower stops get longer. Bubble models must balance deep-stop benefits against that penalty.
- Bubbles come in many sizes: lots of small ones, few large ones. The model sets an **initial critical radius**. Bubbles smaller than it collapse; larger ones are allowed to grow. In the chapter's example graph, a radius of 4 leaves only 3 growing bubbles (two of size 4, one of size 5); a radius of 3 lets 7 bubbles grow. A smaller critical radius means more bubbles allowed to grow, so the model must compensate.
- Each model has two initial critical radii (helium and nitrogen), each tissue has its own set, defaults are empirically derived, and some software lets advanced users change them.

**Ask students:**

1. How do bubble models keep bubbles from growing? _(Keep them below the critical radius using deep stops that maintain pressure.)_
2. Why do deep stops help the fast tissues but hurt the slow tissues? _(Fast tissues off-gas at depth; slow tissues continue on-gassing, lengthening shallow stops.)_
3. If silent bubbles occur on almost every dive, why do bubble models still try to limit them? _(DCS depends on the quantity of free gas, not on bubbles existing.)_
4. A model uses a larger initial critical radius. More or fewer bubbles collapse? _(More collapse, fewer grow, i.e. more conservative.)_
5. Why does a model need separate critical radii for helium and nitrogen? _(The gases diffuse at different rates; each needs its own parameters.)_

## Slides 15–16: VPM and RGBM

**Slide says:** Slide 15: VPM model; dual phase bubble model; how it works; original model; changes made; V-Planner. Slide 16: RGBM; Weinke; how it works.

**Explain VPM (Variable Permeability Model):**

- Developed from the 1970s by Yount and colleagues, who modeled bubble formation in gelatin, and turned into the VPM in 1986 at the University of Hawaii. Its algorithm is freely distributed, which is a big reason it is so popular.
- It tracks gas in **16 tissue compartments** using the Haldane and Schreiner equations, and assumes **gas micronuclei** already exist in blood and tissue before the dive. Its job is to limit bubble growth on ascent.
- _The puzzle:_ by the chapter's own physics, a tiny bubble at the surface (Laplace 0.25 + ambient 1.0 = 1.25 ATA inside) should collapse. So why do micronuclei survive? Answer: a layer of **surfactant** molecules. In very small bubbles these molecules are packed tightly into an impermeable skin, so no gas enters or leaves and the bubble is stable. In larger bubbles they are spread out and gas can pass. Permeability varies with size, hence "Variable Permeability".
- **Original VPM:** never let tissue gas tension exceed the bubble's internal pressure. It worked well for saturation diving but was too conservative for sport and technical diving.
- **Critical volume algorithm:** assumes DCI symptoms come from exceeding a _critical volume of free gas_. It tracks the number of bubbles that grow, their rate of expansion and the time they expand, and recalculates free-gas volume at every stop. Tested for 40–75 m / 130–250 ft.

**Explain RGBM (Reduced Gradient Bubble Model):** Bruce Weinke first extended VPM for repetitive dives, then branched off to build his own dual phase model. Profiles from VPM-B and RGBM are often surprisingly similar, but they differ in three ways:

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
||VPM-B|RGBM|
|Basis of ascent|Bubble volume for the whole ascent|Bubble volume for the initial deep stops, then a traditional dissolved-gas approach for shallow stops|
|Gradient reduction|Boyle's Law compensation|Reduced by three factors: bubbles left from earlier dives, reverse profile dives, extra micronuclei from dives over several days|
|Oxygen window|Not accounted for|Included: the difference between arterial and tissue oxygen pressure|

### Equipment


### Twinset

A **twinset (doubles)** consists of two cylinders connected by a **manifold**, allowing them to function as a single gas supply while maintaining redundancy. Each cylinder has its own valve and first stage regulator, and the manifold includes a central isolation valve.

As soon as you breathe from either regulator Pressure in both cylinders instantly equalizes through the manifold. Although your primary regulator may be physically attached to the **right cylinder**, it is actually drawing gas from **both cylinders simultaneously** because the manifold keeps both sides at the same pressure.

This is why a manifolded twinset is often described as behaving like **one large cylinder**.

If one regulator fails (for example, a free-flow), you can shut down only that side while keeping access to gas through the other regulator. This is the main safety advantage of a manifolded twinset.

### Wings

A donut wing has a **continuous circular (toroidal) air bladder**, allowing gas to move freely around the entire wing.

### Pros

- **Excellent gas movement** regardless of body position (head up, horizontal, head down).
- Easier to vent because gas can always migrate toward the dump valve.
- Better suited for technical diving involving restrictions, caves, or wrecks.
- More forgiving if trim changes during the dive.
- Supported by most modern manufacturers and training organizations.

### Cons

- Gas can move from side to side, which may briefly change balance during large body movements.
- Some divers feel the lower section can "push" the bottom of the cylinders slightly forward when fully inflated (usually only noticeable on the surface).


A horseshoe wing has an **open top**, so air cannot travel directly around the bottom of the wing.

### Pros

- Less gas beneath the cylinders, which some divers believe improves trim with certain cylinder configurations.
- Can intentionally trap gas on one side, which a small number of experienced cave divers have used to compensate for asymmetrical stage bottle configurations.
- Traditionally popular before donut wings became widespread.

### Cons

- Air can become trapped on one side.
- Requires more deliberate positioning to vent completely.
- Can make buoyancy management more difficult when inverted or steeply head-down.
- Few modern manufacturers still produce them.

Today, the overwhelming majority of divers using twinsets choose:

- **Donut wing**
- **Single bladder**
- **40–60 lb lift**, depending on exposure suit and cylinders
